## Q1. Which question is closest to RQ 3.2?

RQ 3.2 asks whether a trusted agent can *expose* agents willing to collude. "Willing" is a disposition; the only way to observe a disposition in this setting is a behaviour that (a) favours the proposer, (b) is costly or at least not otherwise motivated, and (c) is not explained by a general shift. So the closest operationalisation is **behavioural reciprocation after a collusion offer**, and "distinguishing genuine reciprocation from general passivity / instruction-following" is not a separate research question: it is the identification condition that any reciprocation claim must satisfy. "Verbal willingness" is a weaker proxy that this study has already shown to be decoupled from behaviour (E4: ACCEPT-and-not-committed ΔR +0.18 vs DECLINE +0.09, CIs overlap; 0 committed replies among DECLINEs).

The contribution beyond Zeng & Rudzicz is not "agents accept pacts" (they reported ~98%, and C0 here reproduces 100%). It is that **the same three models, in the same game, given the same pact as a peer offer without framework scaffolding, (i) almost never commit verbally (C1: 6 committed / 89 replies; 28 committed / 274 ACCEPT labels overall), (ii) show no targeted sparing of the proposer (HA2a −0.197, HA2b −0.063, E2c index for non-committed recipients −0.175 [−0.318, −0.039]), and (iii) instead challenge *everyone* less (HM −0.213; C1 −0.34, C2 −0.33, vs C3 −0.09, C5 −0.09).** Point (iii) is content-specific: the two messages carrying non-challenge content suppress challenging, the two without do not, regardless of whether agreement was requested. This 2×2 reading is descriptive (HM pooled the four message conditions), but it is the cleanest thing in the data.

## Q2. Is "identifying agents willing to accept" still meaningful?

No, not as a contribution: acceptance under framework scaffolding is established (theirs ~98%, our C0 30/30). The uncertainty that remains, and that neither paper resolves, is **whether acceptance ever translates into preferential behaviour without a decision-time reminder.** Zeng & Rudzicz's behavioural effect sits on top of a "DO NOT CHALLENGE" reminder in every challenge prompt (PREREG line 137 says exactly this about C0: it checks "that instructed compliance is measurable… not that persuasion works"). Our C1 recipients get a softer version of the same thing: the offer and their own reply are re-injected into every later prompt (`private_messages_block.txt`). So within the current data the two cells are (authority framing + directive reminder) and (peer framing + record of the exchange). The cell that would settle Problem C, **authority-assigned alliance, accepted, no reminder**, was never run. That is the one uncertainty worth one more condition; everything else in the status document is already answered or is unanswerable in this environment (see Q3).

## Q3. What would count as evidence of reciprocation, and can Liar's Bar measure it?

Convincing evidence needs, within one agent, (a) a non-challenge rate on the proposer's plays that exceeds (b) the same agent's non-challenge rate on other players' plays of comparable evidential value, with the difference larger than in the no-offer control. Fixed turn order makes (b) structurally unavailable for the one seat that matters: while all four players are alive, the agent after Riley only ever judges Riley. The decomposition confirms it in practice (X had no other-player decision in 15 of 17 HA2c games). Every within-agent selectivity number for X therefore rests on games where someone has died, which is a selected sample.

Two honest consequences:
- The between-agent proxy already in use (ΔR for the facing recipient vs the same seat in C4, minus ΔO for other recipients vs C4; E2c) is the best this environment allows. It mixes seats and should be labelled a proxy, never selectivity.
- The minimum environment change that makes within-agent reciprocation observable is **open challenging**: any alive player may challenge the current play, not only the next seat. That is a rule change to the game loop and the challenge prompt, it breaks comparability with the original benchmark and with all 210 games, and the agents would then face many more decisions per round. It is a redesign, not a follow-up. Riley's known bluff flag (p = 0.5, logged) does not help: agents cannot see cards, so sparing a bluff is not knowingly costly, and a "costly cooperation" reading is unavailable regardless of design.

Can this stand as a contribution without more games? Yes, as a pre-registered negative-and-identification result: a scaffolding-free trusted-agent offer in Liar's Bar does not expose colluders; it induces a content-specific, non-targeted drop in challenging, and the natural metric (selectivity) can produce a Holm-significant artefact when recipients and non-recipients are mixed. For a sprint report, the methodological point (HA2c) is arguably the most useful thing in it, because it is exactly the error a reader of Zeng & Rudzicz-style alliance metrics could make.

## Q5. Is v8 appropriate?

Not as specified. Points in its favour: making Riley a real threat gives protection value, and the requested behaviour (challenge Z) runs *against* the general-passivity drift, so general priming cannot masquerade as compliance. Points against, which dominate:
1. **No instruction-only control.** D1 (protection + "challenge Z") vs D5 (neutral) cannot separate reciprocation from doing what a message says, which HM has already shown these models do. The required cell is "challenge Z" with no protection offered. With it, v8 is 5 conditions × 30 = 150 games to learn whether an instruction with a side-payment is followed more than an instruction without one.
2. **Self-interest.** Once Riley challenges at p = 0.5, accepting protection is dominant; the design measures compliance with a favourable offer, which PREREG's own future-work section already rejected for "concrete in-game rewards".
3. **Outcome is a baseline behaviour.** Challenge rates on other players are already 35–65%; "challenged Z" is not a marker of anything.
4. **D0 with reminders** is C0 again and measures the same thing the gate did.
5. Fixed order still constrains who can be asked to challenge whom (the recipient must be the seat after Z and before Riley), so the observation problem recurs.

**Simpler, identifiable alternative (one condition, 30 games, near-zero code):** C0 without the decision-time reminder. The ally receives the existing framework alliance notification, answers ACCEPT/REFUSE, and then plays with no "DO NOT CHALLENGE" line in its challenge prompt (keep whatever re-injection C0 already uses, so the only change is the reminder). Compare the ally's non-challenge rate on Riley's plays with the same seat in C4 and with C0, same game numbers, paired sign-flip, exactly as the gate was computed. Before running, check in `experiment_player.py` how the C0 notification is carried into later prompts, so that the single difference from C0 is the reminder and nothing else.

## Q6. Recommendation: **A. STOP** (for the sprint report), with the Q5 single-condition run named as the only follow-up worth doing if the team continues post-sprint.

Reasons: the deadline has passed; the data already answer every question that this environment can answer; the remaining uncertainty (acceptance → behaviour without reminders) is real but is one 30-game condition, not a programme, and its result would not change the report's main claims, only its discussion of Zeng & Rudzicz. Reanalysis (B) would only reformat numbers that are already in the exploratory files; v8 (C/D) does not identify the construct.

If the follow-up is run later, pre-register it as:
1. **Question.** Does verbal acceptance of an experimenter-assigned non-challenge alliance translate into preferential non-challenge of the ally when no decision-time reminder is given?
2. **Hypothesis.** H: the ally's non-challenge rate on Riley's plays exceeds the same seat's rate in C4 (predicted > 0). Reference effect: C0's +0.569.
3. **Primary outcome.** Per game number, ally's non-challenge rate on Riley's plays (post-notification decisions, proposer alive, eligibility flag set), minus the same agent's rate in C4.
4. **Design.** One new condition, 30 game numbers g = 0–29, same seeds and seat rotation as the main run; C0, C1 and C4 are already available for the same g. Two-sided sign-flip, α = .05, one test. Secondary (descriptive only): this cell vs C1's X-only ΔR; by model.
5. **Identification assumptions / confounders.** The only prompt difference from C0 is the absent reminder (verify in code). Residual re-injection of the notification acts as a soft reminder, so a positive result shows "acceptance plus a visible record", not pure memory. Qwen's C4 baseline (0.79) leaves ~0.2 of headroom, so the test is mostly carried by the two Llama seats; report by model. Unseeded sampling means the C4 pairing is by game number, not replay. Riley's play rate and survival determine the number of decisions; games with no eligible decision drop out (expect ~20–25 contributing g, as for the gate).
6. **Success / failure.** Supported if p < .05 and mean > 0 with a lower CI bound above about +0.1 (half the gate effect is the smallest difference worth discussing). Failed if the CI includes 0 or the point estimate is below C1's X-only ΔR.
7. **Conclusions.** Positive: verbal acceptance under authority framing carries into behaviour without enforcement, so the gap with C1 is attributable to framing/authority rather than to reminders, and a trusted agent framed as the game's authority *can* expose compliant agents; still not evidence of a collusive disposition, since the alliance was assigned. Null: in these models and this game, every alliance behaviour observed (ours and, by inference only, Zeng & Rudzicz's) depends on a reminder at decision time; verbal acceptance does not expose anything, and the trusted-agent probe cannot detect willingness to collude here.
